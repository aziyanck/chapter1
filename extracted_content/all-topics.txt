TOPIC 1: WIRELESS COMMUNICATION TECHNOLOGY
======================================================================
Principles, Components, Types of Wireless Comm, Networks & Applications

Key Concepts:
  - Definition: Transmitting data without physical cables using electromagnetic waves
  - Basic Principle: Converting info into signals & sending through air via Tx, Channel, and Rx
  - 4 Core Components: Transmitter, Antenna, Channel, and Receiver
  - 7 Types: Radio, Microwave, Satellite, Infrared, Cellular, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth
  - 4 Network Categories: WPAN, WLAN, WMAN, and WWAN
  - Real-world Applications: Cellular, Wi-Fi, GPS, Satellite, IoT Smart-home & Emergency

--- 1. Definition of Wireless Communication  (Foundation & Core Concept) ---
Key Points:
  - Wireless communication is the process of transmitting information between two or more devices without using physical cables.
  - It uses electromagnetic waves, such as radio waves, to transfer data across free space.
  - Enables flexible, untethered mobility for voice, data, video, and control signals.
Examples:
  - Wi-Fi
  - Bluetooth
  - Mobile Networks (4G/5G)
  - Satellite Communication

--- 2. Basic Principle of Wireless Communication  (Signal Conversion & Step-by-Step Flow) ---
Summary: Wireless communication works by converting information into signals and transmitting them through the air.
Steps:
  1. Transmitter — Converts information into electrical signals.
  2. Transmitting antenna — Sends electromagnetic waves through the air.
  3. Wireless channel — Carries the signals through unguided space to the destination.
  4. Receiving antenna — Captures and intercepts the transmitted waves.
  5. Receiver — Converts the received signals back into original information.

--- 3. Components of a Wireless Communication System  (The 4 Essential Hardware & Media Building Blocks) ---
Components:
  - Transmitter — Sends the information by processing, encoding, and modulating the source data into RF energy.
  - Antenna — Transducer that transmits and receives electromagnetic waves into or from free space.
  - Channel — The physical medium (air / atmosphere / vacuum) through which the electromagnetic signals travel.
  - Receiver — Receives, amplifies, demodulates, and processes the signals back to recognizable output.

--- 4. Types of Wireless Communication  (7 Major Wireless Transmission Technologies) ---
Types:
  - Radio Communication — Uses radio waves to transmit information.; Example: FM radio broadcasting
  - Microwave Communication — Uses high-frequency electromagnetic waves for line-of-sight links.; Example: Point-to-point telecom links
  - Satellite Communication — Uses orbiting satellites to relay signals over vast planetary distances.; Example: Satellite television (DTH)
  - Infrared Communication — Uses infrared light spectrum for direct short-distance transmission.; Example: TV remote controls
  - Cellular Communication — Uses a network of geographical cells and base stations for wide mobility.; Example: 4G LTE and 5G NR
  - Wi-Fi — Provides high-speed wireless local network access to devices.; Example: Connecting a laptop to a home router
  - Bluetooth — Enables low-power, short-range wireless data exchange between devices.; Example: Wireless headphones & smartwatches

--- 5. Types of Wireless Networks  (Geographical Coverage & Classification) ---
Networks:
  - WPAN (Wireless Personal Area Network) — Code: WPAN; Scope: Very short distance (a few meters); Example: Bluetooth, Zigbee (smart peripherals)
  - WLAN (Wireless Local Area Network) — Code: WLAN; Scope: Limited area (home, office, campus); Example: Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11 standards)
  - WMAN (Wireless Metropolitan Area Network) — Code: WMAN; Scope: City or metropolitan scale; Example: WiMAX, city-wide municipal wireless
  - WWAN (Wireless Wide Area Network) — Code: WWAN; Scope: Large nationwide / global geographical areas; Example: Cellular networks (4G/5G), Satellite links

--- 6. Applications of Wireless Communication  (Everyday, Industrial, and Critical Domains) ---
Applications:
  - Mobile Phones & Cellular Networks — Global mobile voice, messaging, and high-speed packet data on the go.
  - Wi-Fi & Internet Access — Broadband connectivity in homes, offices, coffee shops, and universities.
  - Bluetooth Peripherals — Connecting wireless audio, keyboards, mice, wearables, and healthcare sensors.
  - Satellite Communication — Intercontinental broadcasting, maritime vessels, aviation, and remote internet.
  - GPS Navigation — Global positioning satellites providing real-time location and turn-by-turn routing.
  - Wireless Sensors & Smart Home — IoT climate sensors, security cameras, smart lighting, and automated locks.
  - Emergency & Military Communication — Disaster recovery, police/fire radio dispatch, and encrypted tactical field links.

TOPIC 2: ANTENNAS
======================================================================
Antenna Basics, Types, Radiation Pattern, Gain, Directivity, Efficiency, Polarization, Beamwidth, Bandwidth & Aperture

Key Concepts:
  - Antenna: Converts electrical signals into EM waves (transmission) and EM waves into electrical signals (reception)
  - 7 Types: Dipole, Monopole, Loop, Yagi-Uda, Parabolic Reflector, Horn, and Microstrip (Patch)
  - Radiation Pattern: Omnidirectional, Directional, and Isotropic
  - Parameters: Gain, Directivity, Efficiency, Beamwidth, Bandwidth, and Effective Aperture
  - Polarization: Linear, Circular, and Elliptical orientation of the electric field

--- 1. Antenna  (Definition & Examples) ---
Definition: An antenna is a device that converts electrical signals into electromagnetic waves for transmission and electromagnetic waves into electrical signals during reception.
Examples:
  - Mobile Phones
  - Wi-Fi Routers
  - Satellite Dishes

--- 2. Types of Antennas  (7 Common Antenna Structures) ---
Items:
  - Dipole Antenna — Consists of two conductive elements.; Example: Radio communication
  - Monopole Antenna — Consists of a single radiating element.; Example: Mobile and vehicle communication
  - Loop Antenna — Consists of a conductor arranged in a closed loop.; Example: Radio receivers and RFID systems
  - Yagi-Uda Antenna — A directional antenna with a driven element, reflector and directors.; Example: Television reception
  - Parabolic Reflector Antenna — Uses a dish-shaped reflector to concentrate signals in a particular direction.; Example: Satellite communication
  - Horn Antenna — Uses a flared structure to transmit or receive electromagnetic waves.; Example: Radar and microwave communication
  - Microstrip (Patch) Antenna — A compact, flat antenna.; Example: Mobile phones, GPS devices and wireless systems

--- 3. Radiation Pattern  (Strength of Radiation vs Direction) ---
Definition: A radiation pattern is a graphical representation of the strength of radiation in different directions around an antenna.
Types of Radiation Pattern:
  - Omnidirectional — Provides broad coverage around the antenna in a particular plane.
  - Directional — Concentrates radiation in a specific direction.
  - Isotropic — Radiates equally in all directions.

--- 4. Antenna Gain  (Directing Energy Compared to a Reference) ---
Definition: Antenna gain indicates how effectively an antenna directs energy in a particular direction compared with a reference antenna.
Key Points:
  - High gain generally means more concentrated radiation.
  - Gain is commonly measured in dBi or dBd.
  - High-gain antennas are useful for long-distance communication.
  - Zero gain (0 dBi) means the antenna radiates the same as an isotropic antenna, with no concentration of energy in any direction.

--- 5. Directivity  (Concentration of Radiation vs an Isotropic Radiator) ---
Definition: Directivity measures how strongly an antenna concentrates its radiation in a particular direction compared with an isotropic radiator.
Key Points:
  - High directivity means radiation is more concentrated in a particular direction.
  - It does not account for antenna losses.
  - Gain = Efficiency × Directivity (G = η · D), so gain includes losses but directivity does not.
Formula:
  D = 4π · U_max / P_rad
  where: U_max = maximum radiation intensity; P_rad = total radiated power

--- 6. Antenna Efficiency  (Radiated Power vs Input Power) ---
Definition: Antenna efficiency is the ratio of the power radiated by an antenna to the power supplied to it.
Formula:
  η = (P_rad / P_in) × 100%
  where: P_rad = power radiated by the antenna; P_in = input power supplied to the antenna
Example: If an antenna receives 100 W of input power and radiates 80 W, its radiation efficiency is 80%.

--- 7. Antenna Polarization  (Orientation of the Electric Field) ---
Definition: Polarization describes the orientation and movement of the electric field of an electromagnetic wave.
Types of Polarization:
  - Linear — The electric field oscillates along a straight line (vertical or horizontal).; Example: FM/AM radio, mobile towers (vertical)
  - Circular — The electric field rotates in a circular path with constant strength (RHCP or LHCP).; Example: GPS, satellite TV, RFID
  - Elliptical — The electric field traces an elliptical path. Linear and circular are special cases of elliptical.; Example: Most practical antennas
Why Polarization Matters:
  - The antenna's shape and orientation decide the polarization: a vertical rod sends vertically polarized waves.
  - The receiving antenna must match the transmitter's polarization, otherwise signal is lost (polarization mismatch loss).
Polarization Mismatch:
| Transmitter | Receiver           | Result                      |
|-------------|--------------------|-----------------------------|
| Vertical    | Vertical           | Full signal                 |
| Vertical    | Tilted 45°         | Half the power lost         |
| Vertical    | Horizontal         | Almost no signal            |
| Circular    | Linear (any angle) | Half the power lost         |

--- 8. Beamwidth  (Angular Width of the Main Lobe) ---
Definition: Beamwidth is the angular width of an antenna's main radiation lobe.
Narrow vs Wide Beamwidth:
  - Narrow Beamwidth — Concentrates radiation in a smaller angular region.
  - Wide Beamwidth — Covers a larger angular region.
Example: Satellite dishes generally have narrow beamwidths, while Wi-Fi router antennas have wide beamwidths to cover an entire room.

--- 9. Antenna Bandwidth  (Usable Range of Operating Frequencies) ---
Definition: Antenna bandwidth is the range of frequencies over which an antenna meets specified performance requirements.
Formula:
  BW = f_H − f_L
  where: f_H = highest operating frequency; f_L = lowest operating frequency

--- 10. Effective Aperture  (Power-Capturing Area of a Receiving Antenna) ---
Definition: Effective aperture represents an antenna's ability to capture power from an incoming electromagnetic wave.
Key Points:
  - It is measured in square metres (m²).
  - Effective aperture is usually smaller than the physical aperture, because part of the incoming power is lost or not captured.
Physical vs Effective Aperture:
  - Physical Aperture — The actual physical area of the antenna that faces the incoming wave (e.g. the full surface of a dish).
  - Effective Aperture — The equivalent area that actually captures power from the wave. Depends on gain and wavelength, not just size.
Formula:
  A_e = G · λ² / 4π
  where: G = linear antenna gain; λ = wavelength

Formulas Summary:
  Directivity: D = 4π · U_max / P_rad
    (U_max is the maximum radiation intensity and P_rad is the total radiated power.)
  Antenna Efficiency: η = (P_rad / P_in) × 100%
    (Ratio of the power radiated by an antenna to the power supplied to it.)
  Antenna Bandwidth: BW = f_H − f_L
    (f_H is the highest and f_L is the lowest operating frequency.)
  Effective Aperture: A_e = G · λ² / 4π
    (G is the linear antenna gain and λ is the wavelength.)

Exam Tips:
  - Define antenna gain, directivity and efficiency, and explain how they differ.
  - An antenna receives 100 W and radiates 80 W — its efficiency is 80%.

TOPIC 3: PROPAGATION MODES
======================================================================
Ground Wave, Sky Wave, Line-of-Sight Propagation & Factors Affecting Propagation

Key Concepts:
  - Propagation modes: The different ways EM waves travel from a transmitting antenna to a receiving antenna
  - Ground wave: Travels along the Earth's surface, useful at LF and MF
  - Sky wave: Refracted back to Earth by the ionosphere, HF band (3–30 MHz)
  - Line-of-sight: Direct path between antennas, used for VHF, UHF and microwave
  - Radio horizon extends beyond the optical horizon due to atmospheric refraction
  - Factors: Frequency, distance, atmosphere, obstacles, ground conductivity, antenna height, power and interference

--- 1. Propagation Modes  (How Radio Waves Travel from Tx to Rx) ---
Definition: Propagation modes are the different ways electromagnetic waves travel from a transmitting antenna to a receiving antenna.
Key Points:
  - Radio waves can travel along the Earth's surface, through the atmosphere, or directly between antennas.
The Main Propagation Modes:
  - Ground Wave — Travels along the Earth's surface.
  - Sky Wave — Refracted back to Earth by the ionosphere.
  - Line-of-Sight — Travels directly between antennas.

--- 2. Ground-Wave Propagation  (Along the Earth's Surface) ---
Definition: Ground-wave propagation is the transmission of radio waves along the Earth's surface.
Working:
  - Radio waves travel close to and follow the Earth's surface.
  - The waves can bend around the Earth's curvature to some extent.
  - Propagation depends on frequency and ground conductivity.
Facts:
  - Frequency: Particularly useful at lower frequencies, such as LF and MF.
  - Applications: AM broadcasting at suitable frequencies, maritime communication and navigation systems.
Advantages:
  - Can cover beyond the visual horizon.
  - Useful for communication over the Earth's surface.
Disadvantages:
  - Signal strength decreases with distance.
  - Ground absorption can weaken the signal.
  - Less suitable for high-frequency transmission over long distances.

--- 3. Sky-Wave Propagation  (Refracted Back by the Ionosphere) ---
Definition: Sky-wave propagation is the transmission of radio waves that travel toward the ionosphere and are refracted back toward the Earth.
Working:
  1. Upward transmission — The transmitting antenna sends radio waves upward.
  2. Enters the ionosphere — The waves enter the ionosphere, a region of the upper atmosphere containing charged particles.
  3. Refraction — Under suitable conditions, the waves are refracted back toward Earth.
  4. Long distance — The signals can travel long distances, sometimes through multiple hops.
Facts:
  - Frequency: Commonly associated with the HF band (3–30 MHz).
  - Applications: Shortwave broadcasting, amateur radio and long-distance radio communication.
Advantages:
  - Enables communication over very long distances.
  - Can reach beyond the line of sight.
Disadvantages:
  - Depends on ionospheric conditions.
  - Signal strength and quality may vary.
  - Communication can be affected by solar activity and atmospheric changes.

--- 4. Line-of-Sight (LOS) Propagation  (Direct Path Between Antennas) ---
Definition: Line-of-sight propagation occurs when radio waves travel along a direct path between the transmitting and receiving antennas.
Working:
  - The transmitting antenna sends radio waves toward the receiver.
  - The receiver must be within a suitable radio path, with sufficient clearance from obstacles.
  - The Earth's curvature limits the distance between antennas near the ground.
  - Radio horizon: the maximum distance for a direct radio path. It extends slightly beyond the optical (visual) horizon because the atmosphere bends radio waves, and increases with antenna height.
Facts:
  - Frequency: Commonly used for VHF, UHF and microwave communication.
  - Applications: Television broadcasting, mobile communication, Wi-Fi links, microwave links and satellite communication.
Advantages:
  - Supports high data rates with suitable equipment.
  - Provides relatively predictable propagation when the path is clear.
  - Suitable for directional communication links.
Disadvantages:
  - Buildings, hills and other obstacles can block or weaken signals.
  - Coverage is limited by the radio horizon.
  - Often requires suitable antenna placement.

--- 5. Factors Affecting Propagation Modes  (What Changes How Radio Waves Travel) ---
8 Key Factors:
  - Frequency — Different frequencies behave differently during propagation.
  - Distance — Greater distances generally cause greater signal loss.
  - Atmospheric Conditions — Temperature, humidity and ionospheric conditions can affect radio waves.
  - Obstacles — Buildings, mountains and trees may block or weaken signals.
  - Ground Conductivity — Affects ground-wave propagation.
  - Antenna Height — Influences line-of-sight coverage.
  - Transmitted Power — Affects the received signal strength.
  - Interference — Other signals can affect communication quality.

Exam Tips:
  - Compare ground wave, sky wave and line-of-sight propagation by frequency, working and applications.
  - Explain why the radio horizon extends beyond the optical horizon.

TOPIC 4: FADING IN MOBILE ENVIRONMENT
======================================================================
Causes, Multipath, Path Loss, Shadowing, Doppler, Types of Fading & Effects

Key Concepts:
  - Fading: Variation in received signal strength over time, distance or frequency
  - Causes: Multipath, movement, obstacles, atmospheric conditions and interference
  - Path loss reduces power with distance; shadowing is caused by obstacles
  - Doppler shift: f_d = (v / λ) cos θ, caused by relative movement
  - Types: Large/small-scale, fast/slow, Rayleigh/Rician

--- 1. Fading  (Definition & Core Idea) ---
Definition: Fading is the variation in the strength of a received signal over time, distance or frequency in a wireless communication system.
Key Points:
  - It occurs due to obstacles, movement of mobile devices and multipath propagation.
Example: While talking on a mobile phone, the signal may become weak when you move behind a building.

--- 2. Causes of Fading  (Why the Received Signal Varies) ---
5 Causes:
  - Multipath Propagation — Signals reach the receiver through different paths due to reflection, diffraction and scattering.
  - Movement of Mobile Devices — Movement changes the signal paths and their relative phases.
  - Obstacles — Buildings, trees and hills can block or weaken signals.
  - Atmospheric Conditions — Changes in the environment can affect radio-wave propagation.
  - Interference — Multiple signal components may combine and strengthen or weaken the received signal.

--- 3. Multipath Propagation  (One Signal, Many Paths) ---
Definition: Multipath propagation occurs when a transmitted signal reaches the receiver through multiple paths.
Key Points:
  - The signals may arrive at different times and with different phases.
How the Copies Combine:
  - Constructive Interference — Signals combine and strengthen the received signal.
  - Destructive Interference — Signals combine and weaken the received signal.
Important:
  - Multipath propagation is a major cause of fading in mobile communication.

--- 4. Path Loss  (Power Reduction with Distance) ---
Definition: Path loss is the reduction in signal power as the distance between the transmitter and receiver increases.
Example: A mobile phone generally receives a weaker signal when it moves farther away from a cell tower, assuming other conditions remain comparable.
Important Difference:
  - Path loss describes the overall reduction in signal power with distance, whereas fading describes signal variations around the expected signal level.

--- 5. Shadowing  (Obstacles Blocking the Signal) ---
Definition: Shadowing occurs when obstacles such as buildings, hills or trees obstruct radio waves and cause variations in the received signal strength.
Key Points:
  - Shadowing is also called shadow fading.
Example: A mobile signal becomes weaker when a user enters a building.

--- 6. Doppler Effect and Doppler Shift  (Frequency Change Due to Movement) ---
Definition: The Doppler effect is the change in the observed frequency of a signal due to relative movement between the transmitter and receiver.
Key Points:
  - Greater movement speed generally produces a larger maximum Doppler shift.
Formula:
  f_d = (v / λ) · cos θ
  where: f_d = Doppler frequency shift; v = relative speed; λ = wavelength; θ = angle between the direction of motion and the direction of wave arrival
Example: When a person moves while using a mobile phone, the received radio signal may experience a frequency shift.

--- 7. Large-Scale vs Small-Scale Fading  (Types of Fading (A & B)) ---
Types:
  - Large-Scale Fading — Variations in the average received signal power over relatively large distances. Includes path loss (power decreases with distance) and shadow fading (obstacles vary the average strength).
  - Small-Scale Fading — Rapid variations in the received signal strength over short distances or periods. Commonly results from multipath propagation and movement.

--- 8. Fast vs Slow Fading  (Types of Fading (C & D)) ---
Types:
  - Fast Fading — The received signal changes rapidly over a short period. Cause: rapid changes in signal paths and relative phases, often associated with movement and Doppler effects.; Example: A moving mobile user experiences rapid signal fluctuations.
  - Slow Fading — The received signal changes more gradually over time. Cause: changes in shadowing and the surrounding environment.; Example: A mobile user gradually moving behind a large building experiences a change in signal strength.

--- 9. Rayleigh vs Rician Fading  (Types of Fading (E & F) — Statistical Models) ---
Types:
  - Rayleigh Fading — A statistical model for multipath fading when there is no dominant direct line-of-sight component.; Example: Mobile communication in an urban environment where buildings obstruct the direct path.
  - Rician Fading — A statistical model used when a dominant signal component, often a direct line-of-sight signal, exists alongside scattered multipath components.; Example: A wireless link with a clear direct path and additional reflected signals.

--- 10. Effects of Fading  (Impact on Communication Quality) ---
Disadvantages:
  - Reduces received signal strength.
  - Increases the probability of transmission errors.
  - Can cause dropped calls.
  - Reduces communication reliability.
  - May decrease data transmission speed.

Formulas Summary:
  Doppler Shift: f_d = (v / λ) · cos θ
    (Doppler frequency shift for relative speed v, wavelength λ and arrival angle θ.)

Exam Tips:
  - Differentiate path loss, shadowing and multipath fading with examples.
  - Compare fast vs slow, and Rayleigh vs Rician fading.

TOPIC 6: DIGITAL DATA TO ANALOG SIGNAL
======================================================================
Digital-to-Analog Modulation: BASK, BFSK, PSK & QPSK

Key Concepts:
  - Digital-to-analog conversion: Converting digital data (0s and 1s) into analog signals for transmission
  - BASK: Carrier amplitude changes according to the data
  - BFSK: Carrier frequency changes according to the data
  - PSK: Carrier phase changes according to the data (BPSK, QPSK, DPSK)
  - QPSK: Four phases, each dibit (2 bits) mapped to one phase

--- 1. Digital-to-Analog Conversion  (Definition & Types) ---
Definition: Digital-to-analog conversion is the process of converting digital data (0s and 1s) into analog signals for transmission through a communication medium.
Key Points:
  - It is also called digital-to-analog modulation.
The Important Techniques:
  - BASK — Binary Amplitude Shift Keying
  - BFSK — Binary Frequency Shift Keying
  - PSK — Phase Shift Keying
  - QPSK — Quadrature Phase Shift Keying
Example: A digital modem uses modulation to transmit computer data over a communication channel.

--- 2. Binary Amplitude Shift Keying (BASK)  (Amplitude Changes with the Data) ---
Definition: BASK is a digital modulation technique in which the amplitude of the carrier signal changes according to the digital data.
Working:
  - When the input bit is 1, a carrier signal with a particular amplitude is transmitted.
  - When the input bit is 0, the carrier may have zero or a different amplitude, depending on the scheme.
  - The receiver detects the amplitude to recover the transmitted bits.
Example: Binary Amplitude Shift Keying (BASK):
| Input bit | Carrier amplitude |
|-----------|-------------------|
| 1         | High              |
| 0         | Zero or low       |
Advantages:
  - Simple to implement.
  - Requires relatively simple circuitry.
Disadvantages:
  - Sensitive to noise and amplitude variations.

--- 3. Binary Frequency Shift Keying (BFSK)  (Frequency Changes with the Data) ---
Definition: BFSK is a digital modulation technique in which the frequency of the carrier changes according to the digital data.
Working:
  - Bit 1 is represented by one frequency.
  - Bit 0 is represented by another frequency.
  - The receiver identifies the frequency and recovers the bits.
Example: Binary Frequency Shift Keying (BFSK):
| Input bit | Carrier frequency |
|-----------|-------------------|
| 1         | Higher frequency  |
| 0         | Lower frequency   |
Advantages:
  - Generally less sensitive to amplitude variations than BASK.
  - Can provide reliable communication in noisy environments.
Disadvantages:
  - Can require more bandwidth than some other modulation techniques.

--- 4. Phase Shift Keying (PSK)  (Phase Changes with the Data) ---
Definition: PSK is a digital modulation technique in which the phase of the carrier signal changes according to the digital data.
Working:
  - Different digital symbols are represented by different carrier phases.
  - The receiver detects the phase or phase difference and recovers the transmitted data.
Example: Binary Phase Shift Keying (BPSK):
| Input bit | Carrier phase |
|-----------|---------------|
| 1         | 0°            |
| 0         | 180°          |
Advantages:
  - Good noise performance compared with BASK in many conditions.
  - Efficient use of transmission power.
Disadvantages:
  - Some PSK systems require accurate carrier synchronization.

--- 5. Quadrature Phase Shift Keying (QPSK)  (Four Phases, Two Bits per Symbol) ---
Definition: QPSK is a phase shift keying technique that uses four carrier phases, so each symbol carries 2 bits.
Working:
  - The input bits are grouped into pairs called dibits (00, 01, 11, 10).
  - Each dibit is represented by one of four carrier phases.
  - The receiver detects the phase of each symbol and recovers both bits.
Example: QPSK Phase Assignment (Gray coded):
| Dibit | Carrier phase |
|-------|---------------|
| 00    | 45°           |
| 01    | 135°          |
| 11    | 225°          |
| 10    | 315°          |
Advantages:
  - Carries twice as many bits per symbol as BPSK.
  - Needs about half the bandwidth of BPSK for the same bit rate.
Disadvantages:
  - The receiver must distinguish four phases, so it is more complex than BPSK.
  - Requires accurate carrier synchronization.

Exam Tips:
  - Explain BASK, BFSK, PSK and QPSK with their working principles.
  - Compare BASK, BFSK, BPSK and QPSK.

TOPIC 7: ANALOG DATA TO ANALOG SIGNAL
======================================================================
Analog Modulation: AM, FM & PM

Key Concepts:
  - Analog-to-analog conversion: Transmitting analog information using an analog carrier signal
  - AM: Carrier amplitude varies with the message; modulation index m = A_m / A_c
  - FM: Instantaneous frequency varies with the message; β = Δf / f_m
  - PM: Carrier phase varies with the message
  - AM → Amplitude, FM → Frequency, PM → Phase

--- 1. Analog-to-Analog Conversion  (Definition & Types) ---
Definition: Analog-to-analog conversion is the process of transmitting analog information using an analog carrier signal.
Key Points:
  - It is also called analog modulation.
  - The figure shows the two inputs: the message (the information) and the carrier (a plain high-frequency wave that carries no information on its own).
  - The modulator combines them into the modulated signal (AM, FM or PM), shown on the next slides.
The Three Main Techniques:
  - AM — Amplitude Modulation
  - FM — Frequency Modulation
  - PM — Phase Modulation
Example: In conventional AM radio broadcasting, the amplitude of a carrier signal varies according to the audio signal.

--- 2. Amplitude Modulation (AM)  (Carrier Amplitude Follows the Message) ---
Definition: AM is a modulation technique in which the amplitude of the carrier signal varies according to the instantaneous amplitude of the message signal.
Working:
  1. Message input — The message signal, such as audio, is supplied to the modulator.
  2. Modulation — The modulator varies the carrier amplitude according to the message.
  3. Transmission — The modulated signal is transmitted through an antenna.
  4. Demodulation — The receiver demodulates the signal to recover the original audio.
Important Point:
  - In conventional AM, the carrier frequency remains constant while its amplitude varies.
Formula:
Modulation Index:
  m = A_m / A_c
  where: m = modulation index; A_m = peak amplitude of the modulating signal; A_c = amplitude of the unmodulated carrier
  Note: For a single-tone message and conventional AM, m is normally kept at or below 1 to avoid overmodulation.
Advantages:
  - Simple to generate and demodulate.
  - Can be implemented using relatively simple receivers.
Disadvantages:
  - More sensitive to amplitude noise.
  - Conventional AM can be power-inefficient.

--- 3. Frequency Modulation (FM)  (Carrier Frequency Follows the Message) ---
Definition: FM is a modulation technique in which the instantaneous frequency of the carrier varies according to the message signal.
Working:
  1. Message input — The message signal is supplied to the modulator.
  2. Modulation — The carrier frequency increases or decreases according to the message.
  3. Transmission — The modulated signal is transmitted.
  4. Demodulation — The receiver detects the frequency variations and recovers the original message.
Important Point:
  - In ideal FM, the carrier amplitude remains constant while its instantaneous frequency varies.
Formula:
FM Modulation Index:
  β = Δf / f_m
  where: β = FM modulation index; Δf = peak frequency deviation; f_m = modulating frequency
Advantages:
  - Better resistance to amplitude noise than conventional AM.
  - Can provide good audio quality.
Disadvantages:
  - Often requires more bandwidth than conventional AM.
  - The transmitter and receiver can be more complex.

--- 4. Phase Modulation (PM)  (Carrier Phase Follows the Message) ---
Definition: PM is a modulation technique in which the phase of the carrier signal varies according to the instantaneous amplitude of the message signal.
Working:
  1. Message input — The message signal is supplied to the modulator.
  2. Modulation — The modulator changes the carrier phase according to the message.
  3. Transmission — The modulated signal is transmitted.
  4. Demodulation — The receiver detects the phase variations to recover the message.
Important Point:
  - In PM, the carrier's phase varies according to the message signal. Its instantaneous frequency also varies when the message phase changes over time.
Advantages:
  - Can provide good resistance to amplitude noise.
  - Forms an important foundation for digital phase modulation.
Disadvantages:
  - Requires suitable phase-modulation and demodulation circuits.
  - The resulting frequency variations depend on how the message changes.

Formulas Summary:
  AM Modulation Index: m = A_m / A_c
    (Peak message amplitude divided by unmodulated carrier amplitude; kept at or below 1.)
  FM Modulation Index: β = Δf / f_m
    (Peak frequency deviation divided by modulating frequency.)

Exam Tips:
  - Explain AM, FM and PM, and compare them.
  - Define modulation index and frequency deviation.

TOPIC 8: ANALOG DATA TO DIGITAL SIGNAL
======================================================================
Sampling, Quantization, Encoding & PCM

Key Concepts:
  - Analog-to-digital conversion: Converting continuous analog information into binary data using an ADC
  - Three steps: Sampling, quantization and encoding
  - Nyquist: f_s ≥ 2·f_max, otherwise aliasing may occur
  - PCM: L = 2^n levels, bit rate R_b = n·f_s (8000 × 8 = 64 kbps)

--- 1. Analog-to-Digital Conversion  (Definition & Main Steps) ---
Definition: Analog-to-digital conversion is the process of converting continuous analog information into digital data represented by binary bits.
Key Points:
  - It is commonly performed using an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC).
The Three Fundamental Steps:
  - Sampling — Measuring the signal at regular time intervals.
  - Quantization — Assigning each sample to a permitted level.
  - Encoding — Representing each level with binary bits.
Example: A microphone captures sound as an analog signal. An ADC converts that signal into digital samples so a computer can process or store it.

--- 2. Sampling  (Measuring the Signal at Regular Intervals) ---
Definition: Sampling is the process of measuring the amplitude of an analog signal at regular time intervals.
Working:
  - The analog signal enters the sampling system.
  - Its amplitude is measured at specific time intervals.
  - These measurements form a sequence of samples.
Example: A digital audio system samples an analog sound signal thousands of times per second.

--- 3. Quantization  (Rounding Samples to Permitted Levels) ---
Definition: Quantization is the process of assigning each sampled amplitude to one of a finite number of permitted amplitude levels.
Working:
  - The sampler produces amplitude measurements.
  - Each measurement is assigned to the nearest permitted quantization level.
  - The result is a sequence of discrete amplitude values.
Important Point:
  - Quantization introduces a difference between the original sample and its assigned level. This difference is called quantization error.
Example: A sampled voltage of 2.3 V might be assigned to a quantization level of 2.25 V.

--- 4. Encoding  (Representing Levels with Binary Bits) ---
Definition: Encoding is the process of representing quantized values using binary bits.
Working:
  - Each quantization level is assigned a binary code.
  - The encoder converts each quantized sample into its corresponding binary code.
  - The binary data can then be stored or transmitted digitally.

--- 5. Pulse Code Modulation (PCM)  (Sampling + Quantization + Encoding) ---
Definition: Pulse Code Modulation (PCM) is a technique that converts an analog signal into digital data through sampling, quantization and encoding.
Working of PCM:
  1. Sampling — Measures the analog signal at regular intervals.
  2. Quantization — Assigns each sample to a permitted amplitude level.
  3. Encoding — Converts each quantized level into a binary code.
  4. Transmission — The binary data is transmitted through a digital communication system.
  5. Reconstruction — At the receiving end, the data is decoded and processed to reconstruct an approximation of the original signal.
Advantages:
  - Supports digital storage and processing.
  - Can provide good noise immunity when signals are regenerated correctly.
  - Works well with digital communication networks.
Disadvantages:
  - Requires bandwidth for transmitting the digital data.
  - Quantization introduces error.
  - ADC and DAC circuits are required when interfacing with analog signals.

Formulas Summary:
  Nyquist Sampling Rate: f_s ≥ 2 · f_max
    (Minimum sampling frequency for ideal reconstruction of a band-limited signal.)
  Quantization Levels: L = 2ⁿ
    (Number of levels L for n bits per sample.)
  PCM Bit Rate: R_b = n · f_s
    (8,000 samples/s × 8 bits = 64 kbps.)

Exam Tips:
  - Explain sampling, quantization and encoding, and PCM with a block diagram.
